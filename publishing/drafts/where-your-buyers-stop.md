# Where Your Buyers Stop

*Draft of 2026-11-11. Not published. Notes for the author are at the end.*

> **Opening scene, yours to write.** Two or three short paragraphs on a moment you lived, where a team copied a playbook that did not fit its buyers. The scene has one job. It shows a leader choosing how to sell before anyone asked why buyers were not buying. End it on the question the next section answers.

## Everyone is hiring the same person

Monthly job listings for forward-deployed engineers rose more than 800% between January and September of 2025, on a figure [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91435680/postings-for-this-ai-job-are-up-800) attributes to the Financial Times. A forward-deployed engineer sits with the customer and makes the product work in the customer's environment. Palantir made the title famous. Now it is on hiring plans across AI.

Some of those companies need them. Their buyers sign, struggle to get the product working, and leave. An engineer on site fixes exactly that.

Many do not. Their buyers never get as far as signing, and an engineer who makes the product work cannot help a buyer that has not decided to buy it. Those teams hired the answer to a question their buyers were not asking.

Outbound email goes the same way. A market goes quiet, so the team sends more. Every other seller in the category does the same, and the buyer's inbox becomes a place where nothing gets read.

In my first post I wrote that you read the defense before you call the play. I also drew a curve to do the reading, and the curve was harder to use than it should have been. This is the simpler version. It starts with one question. Where do your buyers stop?

## Three places a buyer stops

Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna studied 2.5 million recorded sales conversations for [*The JOLT Effect*](https://www.jolteffect.com/) and found that 40 to 60% of qualified deals end in no decision. Most teams treat that as one problem. A buyer can fail to buy from you in three places, and only one of them lands in that bucket.

- **They never look.** The buyer has the problem and never starts an evaluation with anyone.
- **They look and decide nothing.** The buyer takes the demo and likes it, and the deal dies somewhere inside its own organization.
- **They sign and fail to adopt.** The contract closes, the product never becomes part of the work, and the renewal is where you find out.

Each place is a different cost. Economists call what it costs to use a market beyond the price a *transaction cost*. Carl Dahlman sorted those costs into three kinds in 1979, and with small changes they are the three here.

| Where the buyer stops | The cost in the way | The buyer's question |
|---|---|---|
| They never look | Search | Which solutions exist, and which seller can I trust? |
| They look and decide nothing | Consensus | Can my organization agree to this? |
| They sign and fail to adopt | Implementation | Will it work here, and will the seller deliver? |

Product-led and sales-led do not appear in that table. Those labels describe the seller's tool, a trial or a salesperson, and they say nothing about the buyer. I think a way of selling should be named for the cost it carries for the buyer. There are three.

## Economists got here first, for one buyer

I would like to claim this sorting as mine. Most of it is fifty years old.

In 1970 Phillip Nelson split goods by the cheaper way for a buyer to learn their quality. A *search good* is one you can judge by inspecting it before you buy. An *experience good* is one you have to buy and use. A can of tuna is an experience good, because trying one costs almost nothing. Nelson then found, in census and advertising data, that sellers behave differently depending on which kind they sell. They advertise differently, and their markets concentrate differently. A free trial is his experience good with the price of trying set to zero.

Three years later Michael Darby and Edi Karni added a third kind. A *credence quality* is one the buyer cannot judge even after using the product. Their example is car repair. The mechanic who diagnoses the fault also sells the fix, and you cannot tell afterward whether you needed the part. Anything sold on an expert's diagnosis has a credence quality in it.

So the argument between product-led and sales-led is an old one. Let the buyer try it where trying is cheap. Send an expert where the buyer cannot judge alone.

Both papers rest on an assumption that does not survive a hospital. Their buyer is one person, and that person always buys from someone. A B2B buyer is an organization. Frederick Webster and Yoram Wind described it in 1972 as a group of people with different roles and different stakes in one purchase. An organization also has an option that is missing from the seller's list of competitors, which is to do nothing.

That leaves two places where enterprise deals die that sorting by quality never reaches. One is an organization that cannot agree. The other is the labor of making the product work once it arrives, which is more than learning whether it is any good.

None of the pieces is mine. My bet is that you have to read all three costs together, and that the one in the way should decide how you sell.

## What you can see before any deal

You do not have to lose twenty deals to learn where buyers stop. Two things about a market are visible from outside, and they predict it.

**The workflow.** This is the procedure your product changes. Ask two things about it. Is it settled, meaning written down and followed, with its exceptions known? And is it entangled, meaning other things change when it changes?

**The organization.** Count the roles that can say no. The person who runs the workflow, the one who owns it, the one who pays for it and the one who polices it are often four different people.

| What you see | Where more buyers stop | What selling has to do |
|---|---|---|
| The workflow is not settled | They never look | Teach, by giving the buyer a hypothesis about its own workflow |
| The workflow is settled and stands alone | Nowhere in particular | Let the buyer try it |
| The workflow is settled and entangled | They sign and fail to adopt | Prove it will work here, and carry the change |
| The organization is complex | They look and decide nothing | Bring the organization to a decision |

Health tech has all four.

- **Not settled.** Ask ten health systems how they follow up with patients after discharge and you will hear ten processes, some of them invented during the call. A buyer that cannot describe its own procedure does not shop for a product that changes it.
- **Settled, and stands alone.** Text reminders for appointments at a small practice. The front desk can switch them on this afternoon, watch whether no-shows fall, and switch them off if they do not. That is a *plug-in* purchase, one that works on arrival and strands nothing if the buyer walks away.
- **Settled and entangled.** Prior authorization. The steps are known, and each one touches the health record, the payer's rules and the billing team. That is a *built-in* purchase, where someone has to build something that only works here, such as an integration, a changed workflow or retrained staff.
- **A complex organization.** An ambient scribe at a large health system. A physician can love it after one clinic day. The medical informatics lead, security, compliance and finance still each get a say, and each asks what it does to their own budget and standing.

The last row stacks on the others. A built-in purchase at a complex organization has two costs in the way, and you carry both.

## How fashion picks the wrong cost

Go back to the forward-deployed engineers. They carry the implementation cost, so they are the right hire where buyers sign and fail to adopt. Where buyers look and decide nothing, the engineer shows up after the deal has died in a budget meeting the seller was never in.

More outbound carries no cost at all in a loud market. Each message adds to a volume that no sender pays for, so the response to every sender falls, yours included.

A free trial fits a buyer that can decide alone. Put it in front of a complex organization and one person tries the product, likes it, and then has to sell it internally with no help from you.

The classic relay has one rep book the meeting, another close and a third manage the account, each paid on their own stage. That design is right where little is in the way. Everywhere else, nobody in the relay owns the cost that is stopping the buyer.

Two consequences follow, and neither is comfortable.

**You reach only the buyers whose cost you are built to carry.** What your team does sets your addressable market as much as what your product does. Deals that die at the same stage for the same reason are usually outside that reach, and coaching reps harder does not bring them in.

**Pay follows the cost.** Where little is in the way, pay for speed. Where implementation is in the way, part of the pay should wait until the implementation confirms what was sold. That works only once you have defined what a complete discovery looks like. Before that, you are paying a rep on something the rep cannot affect.

## What I do not know yet

Nobody has tested this reading, me included. It comes from watching deals, which makes it a hypothesis.

I can already see three limits.

- **A buyer can stop early.** A leader who knows the organization will fight a change may never take the first call. Your records then show a search problem, and the cause is consensus. So the table compares markets, and it will misplace some buyers.
- **One place is invisible.** Buyers who never looked are in nobody's CRM. You have to go and ask them.
- **Two costs can be in the way at once.** Then you run both, and the mistake is picking one and calling it the strategy.

If you run this on your own book and it comes out wrong, tell me. That is the test I cannot run alone.

## Monday morning

1. **Sort your last twenty lost or stalled deals by where the buyer stopped.** The buyer looked and decided nothing, or it signed and failed to adopt. If most land in one place, you have found your cost.
2. **Call five buyers no seller has met.** They have the problem and never evaluated anyone. Ask what they think buying would take. Nothing else shows you the first stopping place.
3. **Read your market's workflow and organization.** Settled or not, entangled or not, and how many roles can say no. Check the answer against step one, and where the two disagree, write it down. That disagreement is the most useful thing you will learn all week.
4. **Name who does the work today.** For the cost in the way, put a name beside each piece of the work. Someone who could do it does not count.
5. **Question the next copied hire.** Before you open a role because another company did, write down which stopping place it addresses. If you cannot, you are hiring someone else's answer.

Reading your buyers is slower than copying a playbook, and this shit is hard. Your competitors are copying one right now.
