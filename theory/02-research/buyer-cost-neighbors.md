The eight sources from Attewell to Miller and Heiman, and Swait, reached this file through two outside reviews in 2026-11. The mark beside each records how far it was read. Both reviews were run with research tools, mostly at the abstract, and no reader from the marketing transaction-cost line has checked the result.

**Abstract.** TCG says a buyer pays three costs beyond price, search, consensus and implementation, and that the kind of cost in the way decides how a product has to be sold. Many neighbors hold a piece of that. This file sets out what each neighbor claims, where TCG agrees or concedes, and the prediction that separates them. Each prediction is a test a deal or a data set could settle. The file ends with what is the author's own and what is synthesis, and with neighbors nobody has read yet.

**Key claims:**
- Most of TCG is synthesis, and the neighbors below hold the parts.
- Four things look like the author's own: the question asked by kind of cost, the cost a buyer expects against the cost it would meet, Turnkey's erosion, and a seller's reach set by the cost it is built to carry.
- TCG concedes that costs add and that a gain can offset one. Constitution 7.0 dropped the rule that forbade it.
- Each neighbor yields a prediction that differs from TCG's. [06-calibration.md](../01-foundation/06-calibration.md) carries the ones waiting on data, as items 52, 68 and 69.

## Gartner and CEB buyer enablement

Gartner and its CEB predecessor hold that a B2B purchase fails on the buyer's side of the table. The buying tasks are hard, the group cannot agree, and more good information can make it worse. Suppliers win by making the purchase easier. TCG agrees on the diagnosis. Gartner's six buying jobs sort under the three costs. Problem identification, solution exploration and supplier selection are search. Consensus creation is consensus. Requirements building is search where it defines fit and consensus where the roles must agree to it. Validation is the buyer's share of implementation before it commits. The grouping is TCG's reading. Gartner also reports buyers revisiting the jobs in no fixed order (from secondary accounts). Its consensus-creation job, and Schmidt, Adamson and Bird's consensus sale, describe the consensus-led motion, so TCG adds the name and the rule for when it applies.

**The separating prediction.** Gartner prescribes sensemaking, helping the buyer interpret what it has gathered, as a general uplift. TCG predicts it pays where consensus is the cost in the way. Where the group agrees and the open question is whether the product will work in its environment, sensemaking changes little until the seller proves it there.

## Moore: handedness and the Chasm

Moore holds that firms are built either for complex systems, with few customers and large customized deals, or for volume operations, with many customers and standard deals, and that mixing the two fails. In *Crossing the Chasm*, mainstream buyers want a whole product and peer references before they buy. TCG reads volume operations as close to Turnkey, and whole product and references as answers to the implementation question. TCG supplies the mechanism behind Moore's axis, which is the cost in the way.

**The separating predictions.** A low-value product with an entangled workflow, such as a tool that must connect to each small clinic's legacy system, sits in volume operations on Moore's axis. TCG calls it implementation-led and predicts self-serve stalls. On references, TCG predicts that a guarantee, outcome-based fees or a proof in the buyer's environment can stand in for peer references. A seller that crosses to the mainstream on guarantees alone is the test.

## MEDDICC, SPIN and Solution Selling

These are qualification and discovery methods. MEDDICC scores a deal on metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, pain, champion and competition. SPIN holds that questions developing a problem's implications predict success in large sales. Solution Selling has the seller surface latent pain and shape the buyer's vision. TCG agrees that execution matters. MEDDICC's economic buyer and decision process map the buyer's organization, which is the consensus cost, and qualification is the seller's question at Time1 asked of one deal.

**The separating prediction.** Take a built-in purchase with a strong champion, access to the economic buyer, quantified metrics and a mapped process, and nobody able to say whether the product will work in the buyer's environment. MEDDICC scores it qualified. TCG predicts it stalls, and that more work on the champion does not move it.

## Value selling and the Challenger Sale

Anderson, Narus and van Rossum argue that a value proposition should state the few points of difference that matter against the next-best alternative, quantified and documented. Dixon and Adamson argue a Challenger representative teaches an insight that reframes the buyer's problem. TCG agrees that documented value matters. The next-best alternative is the buyer's build or do nothing, and documentation is evidence the buyer can check. [value-and-persuasion.md](./value-and-persuasion.md) covers the evidence.

**The separating prediction.** Take a reframe with no new evidence the buyer can check. Challenger predicts a higher close rate and a higher price. TCG predicts no change in what the buyer captures, and a penalty at renewal where the claim outruns a result the buyer can see. If price rises in matched deals and renewal holds, the second standing assumption fails in that market.

## Product-led and sales-led growth: Skok and Janz

Skok and Janz tie the motion to economics. Contract value must cover acquisition cost, and sales complexity raises that cost steeply, so low-value products go low-touch. TCG concedes the heuristic works and says why. Approval rules in most firms track spend, so a small purchase clears with one signature and a large one calls for a committee. That is claim 8's exception. Product-led sales, where a sales team picks up accounts that usage surfaces, is a search cost the buyer carries alone followed by a consensus or implementation cost the seller carries.

**The separating predictions.** The heuristic breaks where something other than spend drives the consensus cost. A low-value tool that needs a security review and a finance sign-off should go low-touch by Skok and Janz. TCG predicts it stalls unless a marketplace contract or a pre-approved vendor list carries the cost (item 68). Second, TCG predicts the sales-assist stage arrives when vendors enter a category, with buyer size held constant. The usual account says it arrives as sellers move upmarket (item 52).

## Total cost of ownership and Cannon and Homburg

Ellram's total cost of ownership looks beyond price to the acquisition, transaction and post-transaction costs a buyer carries, and sums them to compare suppliers. Cannon and Homburg (2001) show that supplier behaviors lower a customer firm's product, acquisition and operations costs, and that customers buy more from suppliers who lower each. That is the closest academic statement of the premise that a seller wins by carrying part of the buyer's cost.

TCG concedes the sum. Before 7.0 it held that a low total built on one high cost could not clear a buyer. The evidence read cutoffs as steep penalties (Swait), and the author's own account has a hard-to-move organization seeking a gain large enough to justify the effort, so 7.0 dropped the rule. What TCG adds is the earlier question, whether the buyer buys from anyone, and the claim that the kind of cost decides the motion.

**The separating prediction.** Both neighbors model the costs as additive. TCG predicts an interaction: lowering a cost that is not in the way does little until the one in the way falls (item 69).

## Academic channel and buying work

- **Attewell (1992)** finds that one barrier, the know-how to implement, set how business computing was bought, and that shared intermediaries such as service bureaus lowered it until buyers could provide for themselves. It is the nearest ancestor of claim 1, and of claim 4's floor lowered by investment across deals. Attewell does not rank barriers. TCG adds the other two costs and the reading that picks one.
- **Ram and Sheth (1989)** pair each barrier to adopting an innovation with its own remedy. TCG shares the pairing and grounds the barriers in three costs.
- **Bunn (1993)** sorts organizational purchases into six approaches by how much search, analysis, proactive focus and procedural control each uses. The three motions resemble it. Bunn's approaches follow from purchase importance, task uncertainty and buyer power. TCG's motion follows from the cost in the way, so two purchases Bunn would class alike should be sold differently when one is built-in.
- **Rangan, Menezes and Maier (1992)** choose a channel from the functions the customer requires, and **Moriarty and Moran (1990)** give each demand-generation task to the cheapest channel that can do it. TCG shares the logic and divides the work by cost. It predicts that in implementation-led deals, putting the solutions engineer first beats the funnel order.
- **Rackham and DeVincentis (1999)** separate transactional, consultative and enterprise buyers. Transactional buyers sit close to Turnkey. Their "value created" is largely TCG's cost carried, unless they mean the sale makes the product worth more.
- **Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967)** classify purchases as new task, modified rebuy and straight rebuy. A straight rebuy sits near Turnkey. TCG predicts it can still turn consensus-led when a decision role changes hands.
- **Weiss and Heide (1993) and Heide and Weiss (1995)** measure search and switching in technology markets. They split considering new vendors from switching to one, which is finer than TCG's single search cost.
- **Liang and Huang (1998) and Teo and Yu (2005)** show consumers choosing a channel by its transaction cost. TCG adds the B2B organization and the seller's work.
- **Malone, Yates and Benjamin (1987)** argue that information technology moves exchange toward markets by lowering coordination costs, and that specific assets and complex product descriptions hold it in hierarchies. TCG applies the mechanism to one buyer's decision. It predicts the shift stops at a floor, whatever happens to search costs.
- **Sheth (1973)** separates autonomous from joint buying decisions, and **Miller and Heiman (1985)** staff the buying influences a seller has to reach. Both are prior art for decision roles.
- **Aldrich and Fiol (1994)** describe the legitimacy a new industry must win before any firm in it sells easily. That is a floor in claim 4's sense.
- **Hauser and Wernerfelt (1990)** model a consideration set as the options whose expected gain exceeds the cost of evaluating them. That is the nearest mechanism for a buyer that never looks because of the cost it expects.
- **Wernerfelt (1994)** models sales assistance as matching buyers to products, kept honest by the seller's reputation, and **Walton and McKersie (1965)** separate bargaining that creates value from bargaining that divides it. Axiom II's distinction between spending that shows the buyer what it will face and spending that moves money restates both.
- **Gensch (1987) and Gilbride and Allenby (2004)** find organizational choice runs in two stages: a screen on cutoffs, then trade-offs among the survivors. **Swait (2001)** estimates the cutoffs as penalties that respondents breach when another attribute compensates. The premise's line that the costs are not walls takes Swait's reading.

## Hockenmaier

Hockenmaier (2023) applies Dahlman's three costs to marketplaces. A marketplace sells the removal of transaction costs, and its stages each remove another. This is the closest vocabulary match found. TCG adds the buyer's organization, the seller's side and what happens after signature.

**The separating prediction.** His stages imply a fixed order of removal. TCG predicts the order follows the cost in the way, so a services marketplace whose buyers doubt delivery should lead with guarantees before search.

## What is the author's own and what is synthesis

Most of TCG is synthesis. The three costs are Dahlman's. Hold-up and the contract apparatus come from Williamson and the marketing transaction-cost line. The buyer as an organization of roles comes from Webster and Wind and the Gartner work. Winning by carrying the buyer's cost is in Cannon and Homburg. Contract value against acquisition cost is Skok and Janz. Naming the motion for one barrier recombines Attewell, Ram and Sheth, and Bunn. "Price is a transfer" is standard economics, though not standard sales practice.

Four things look like the author's own, as far as the searches reached.

1. **The question, asked by kind of cost.** Transaction cost economics asks whether a firm buys or builds. TCG asks whether the buyer buys, builds or does nothing, reads which of three costs is in the way, and names the motion for it. Product-led and sales-led then name the seller's tool.
2. **The cost a buyer expects against the cost it would meet.** A seller can lower a cost and never show it, and the buyer never looks. Hauser and Wernerfelt supply the mechanism, and applying it to each cost appears new.
3. **Turnkey as a market condition that erodes with entry,** so sales-assist arrives as the vendor count rises with the buyer mix held constant.
4. **A seller's reach set by the cost it is built to carry,** with pay following that cost.

The 2026-11-09 review counted a fifth and ranked it first: a hard gate, where any one cost past a limit kept the buyer out. Constitution 7.0 retired it for the reasons given under total cost of ownership.
