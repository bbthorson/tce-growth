The ten sources from Attewell to Mantrala reached this file through the [2026-11-09 research review](./research-review-2026-11-09.md), which records how far each was read.

**Abstract.** TCG says a buyer pays three costs beyond price to use the market: search and information, bargaining and decision, and policing and enforcement. Each has its own threshold, and ease on one does not buy back another above its threshold. No source this search found has assembled that structure for the B2B buyer's decision. Many neighbors hold a piece of it. This file sets out what each neighbor claims, where TCG agrees or concedes, and the single prediction that separates them. Each prediction is a test a deal or a data set could settle.

**Key claims:**
- Most of TCG is synthesis, and the neighbors below hold the parts. The gate, the expected position against the actual one, Turnkey's erosion and the directional seller limit are its own.
- TCG concedes that costs add. The disagreement with total-cost-of-ownership work is over offset, because TCG predicts a prohibitive cost is not bought back by savings elsewhere.
- Each neighbor yields a prediction that differs from TCG's, listed per neighbor.
- Two weak points the neighbors exposed, the value assumption and the verification framing of future cost, were answered in Constitution 5.0 and are backed by their own research files.

**Supports in TCG:**
- **Axiom I — Law of Transaction Cost Composition** — the non-compensatory rule and the gate are tested against Ellram, Cannon and Homburg, and MEDDICC.
- **Axiom II — Law of Transaction Investment** — the seller who pays down the buyer's cost is the idea Cannon and Homburg and Gartner already state in part.
- **Where the sale starts** ([01-motions.md](../01-foundation/01-motions.md)) — the product-led and sales-led discussion below maps the two motions onto it.

## Gartner and CEB buyer enablement

Gartner and its CEB predecessor hold that the B2B purchase fails on the buyer's side of the table. Completing the buying tasks is hard, the group cannot agree, and more good information can make it worse. Suppliers win by making the purchase easier. TCG agrees on the diagnosis and treats purchase ease as close to the buyer's cost of using the market beyond price. Gartner's finding that buyers prefer to buy without a rep, yet still want a seller's word on whether a product fits their company, is the policing cost in Gartner's terms. Read through TCG, each of Gartner's six buying jobs falls under one of Dahlman's costs. Problem identification, solution exploration and supplier selection are search. Consensus creation is bargaining. Requirements building is search where it defines fit and bargaining where the roles must agree to it. Validation, checking before commitment, is the share of policing the buyer carries before it commits. The grouping is TCG's reading, not Gartner's. Gartner also reports buyers looping back and revisiting the jobs in no fixed order (from secondary accounts), which [01-motions.md](../01-foundation/01-motions.md) section 9 reads as jobs that vary inside fixed stages. Its consensus-creation job, and Schmidt, Adamson and Bird's consensus sale, describe the consensus-led motion. What TCG adds there is the label and the rule that names it.

TCG adds three separately thresholded costs, a rule for where the sale starts, and a view of what the buyer carries after signing. **The separating prediction:** Gartner prescribes sensemaking (helping the buyer interpret the information it has gathered) as a general uplift. TCG predicts it pays only where the bargaining cost binds. In a deal where the group agrees and the open question is "will it work in our environment," sensemaking changes nothing until a seller-funded proof pays the policing cost. Gartner's information-overload result also forces TCG to file overload under the decision cost, or its search instruments over-prescribe content.

## Moore: handedness and the Chasm

Moore holds that firms are built either for complex systems (few customers, large customized deals) or for volume operations (many customers, standardized deals), and that mixing the two fails. In *Crossing the Chasm*, mainstream buyers want a whole product and peer references before they buy. TCG reads volume operations as close to Turnkey, the condition where every cost is low enough for the buyer to pay alone. It reads whole product and references as policing instruments. TCG supplies the mechanism behind the axis: which cost binds, and how far it sits from its threshold.

**The separating predictions:** A low-value product with a high policing cost, such as a tool that must connect to each small clinic's legacy system, sits in volume operations on Moore's axis. TCG calls it implementation-led and predicts self-serve stalls. On references, TCG predicts a guarantee, outcome-based fees or a seller-funded proof in the buyer's environment can stand in for peer references. A seller that crosses to the mainstream on guarantees alone is the test.

## MEDDICC, SPIN and Solution Selling

These are qualification and discovery methods. MEDDICC scores a deal on metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, pain, champion and competition. SPIN holds that questions developing a problem's implications predict success in large sales. Solution Selling has the seller surface latent pain and shape the buyer's vision. TCG agrees that execution matters. MEDDICC's economic buyer and decision process map a coalition, which is the bargaining and decision cost. Qualification is TCG's seller condition asked of one deal.

TCG adds a gate across all three costs. MEDDICC has no slot for an over-threshold "will it work here" that kills a deal outright. **The separating prediction:** a deal with a strong champion, access to the economic buyer, quantified metrics and a mapped process, but an implementation cost above the buyer's threshold this year. MEDDICC scores it qualified. TCG predicts no decision whatever the champion does. SPIN and Solution Selling also share the value-moving conflict covered next.

## Value selling and the Challenger Sale

Anderson, Narus and van Rossum argue that a value proposition should state the few points of difference that matter against the next-best alternative, quantified and documented. Dixon and Adamson argue a Challenger rep teaches an insight that reframes the buyer's problem so the supplier's strength becomes valuable. TCG agrees that documented value matters. The next-best alternative is the reference point in the buyer's condition, and documentation is a seller investment that lowers the buyer's information and policing costs. [Value and Persuasion](./value-and-persuasion.md) covers value in depth. The point here is the neighbor comparison.

TCG relocates each value instrument onto a cost, and adds the buying coalition and post-sale exposure. **The separating prediction:** a reframe with no new verifiable evidence. Challenger predicts a higher close rate and a higher price. TCG predicts no change in what the buyer realizes, and exposure later where the claim outruns the result. If price realization rises in matched deals, TCG's assumption that sellers cannot move willingness to pay fails in that market. TCG must name in advance what would count against it, or re-describing every value effect as an information cost makes the assumption unfalsifiable.

## Product-led and sales-led growth: Skok and Janz

Skok and Janz tie motion to economics. Contract value must cover acquisition cost, and sales complexity raises that cost steeply, so low-value products go low-touch. TCG concedes the heuristic works, and says why. Approval rules in most firms track spend, so price causes bargaining cost. That is a bargaining cost floor. A small purchase clears with one signature and a large one calls for a committee. Product-led sales, where a sales team picks up accounts that usage surfaces, is TCG's ordering of a self-serve search cost followed by a seller-paid bargaining or policing cost.

The heuristic breaks where something other than spend drives the bargaining cost. **The separating prediction:** a low-value tool that needs a security review and finance sign-off. Skok and Janz predict low touch. TCG predicts it stalls unless a channel pays the cost, such as a marketplace contract or a pre-approved vendor list. A second test: the sales-assist stage arrives when vendors enter a category, even with buyer size held constant, where the hybrid discourse says it arrives only as sellers move upmarket.

## Total cost of ownership: Ellram

Ellram's total cost of ownership looks beyond a purchase's price to the acquisition, transaction and post-transaction costs the buyer carries, and sums them to compare suppliers. TCG concedes the sum is right for choosing among vendors that are all viable, and for small buyers where one payer carries all three costs and one capacity covers them. TCG adds the thresholds and forbids offset, so the costs are non-compensatory. They add, but a low total built on one prohibitive cost does not clear the buyer.

**The separating prediction:** a procurement team running total cost of ownership ranks a supplier with a low total and one prohibitive cost above a supplier with a higher, even total. TCG predicts the first deal does not happen.

## Cannon and Homburg

Cannon and Homburg (2001) show that supplier behaviors lower a customer firm's product, acquisition and operations costs, and that customers expand purchases from suppliers who lower each. This is the closest academic statement of Axiom II: the seller wins by investing to lower the buyer's cost beyond price. TCG adds the buying coalition, the gate, the order of instruments, the seller's own condition and hold-up. They model the effects as additive. **The separating prediction:** an interaction. Lowering one cost does little while another reads past its threshold. Survey data of their kind can test this directly.

## Academic channel and buying work

- **Rangan, Menezes and Maier (1992)** choose a channel from the functions the customer requires. TCG agrees on the logic and grounds the functions in three costs. Mostly synthesis. TCG predicts the order of instruments can differ deal by deal for one product.
- **Moriarty and Moran (1990)** assign each demand-generation task to the cheapest channel that can do it. TCG shares the division of labor and divides it by cost rather than funnel task. TCG predicts that in implementation-led deals, putting the solutions engineer first beats the funnel order.
- **Rackham and DeVincentis (1999)** separate transactional, consultative and enterprise buyers, and say the sales force must create value. TCG shares the segmentation, and transactional buyers sit close to Turnkey. The conflict is possibly terminological. Their "value created" is largely TCG's cost reduced, unless they mean the product is worth more to the buyer because of the sale.
- **Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967)** classify purchases as new task, modified rebuy and straight rebuy. A straight rebuy sits near Turnkey. TCG predicts a straight rebuy can still turn consensus-led when a decision role changes hands.
- **Weiss and Heide (1993) and Heide and Weiss (1995)** measure organizational search and switching in technology markets. They split consideration of new vendors from switching, which is finer than TCG's single search cost, since a buyer can consider widely and still not switch.
- **Liang and Huang (1998) and Teo and Yu (2005)** show consumers choosing a channel by its transaction cost. This is a consumer-side precursor. TCG adds the B2B coalition, three costs and seller investment. Synthesis.
- **Malone, Yates and Benjamin (1987)** argue that information technology moves exchange toward markets by lowering coordination costs, and that asset specificity and complex product descriptions hold it in hierarchies. TCG shares the mechanism and applies it to one buyer's participation decision rather than to the form of a whole market. Synthesis. TCG predicts the shift stops where a policing cost floor binds, whatever happens to search costs.
- **Bunn (1993)** sorts organizational purchases into six buying decision approaches by how much search, analysis, proactive focus and procedural control each uses. The search-led, consensus-led and implementation-led split resembles it, and the [2026-11 adversarial review](./adversarial-review-2026-11.md) charges TCG with renaming it. The difference is the cause. Bunn's approaches follow from purchase importance, task uncertainty and buyer power. TCG's motion follows from the tightest cost, so TCG predicts that two purchases Bunn would class alike start differently when one has a binding policing cost.
- **Attewell (1992)** finds that one barrier, the know-how to implement, set how business computing was bought, and that shared intermediaries such as service bureaus lowered it until buyers could provide for themselves. That is the closest published statement of naming the motion for the binding cost, and of a cost floor lowered by investment across deals. TCG reads it as its nearest ancestor, though Attewell does not rank barriers. TCG adds the other two costs, the thresholds and the rule that picks one.
- **Ram and Sheth (1989)** pair each barrier to adopting an innovation with its own remedy, functional and psychological. TCG shares the pairing and grounds the barriers in three costs a buyer pays to use the market.
- **Sheth (1973)** separates autonomous from joint industrial buying decisions and models how the conflict in joint ones is resolved, and **Miller and Heiman (1985)** staff the buying influences a seller has to reach. Both are prior art for the bargaining cost's decision roles.
- **Aldrich and Fiol (1994)** describe the legitimacy a new industry has to win before any firm in it can sell easily, a hurdle every entrant shares. That is a cost floor in TCG's terms. The per-cost floor tied to a participation threshold, and the split between floor and category prior, are TCG's own.
- **Hauser and Wernerfelt (1990)** model a consideration set as the options whose expected gain exceeds the cost of evaluating them. That is the nearest mechanism for a buyer whose expected position keeps it out of discovery, though there the bar rises with the expected gain, a compensatory screen, and the gate's does not. Applying it to each transaction cost appears new.
- **Wernerfelt (1994)** models sales assistance as matching buyers to products, kept honest by the seller's reputation, and two of **Walton and McKersie's (1965)** four subprocesses are integrative and distributive bargaining, creating value against dividing it. Axiom II's two jobs for seller spending, raising what the deal creates or letting it go ahead with checkable evidence, against moving cost from one side to the other, restate both. Its contribution is the accounting test in open question 45.
- **Lodish (1971)** allocates call time to maximize contribution from response functions the salespeople judge, and **Mantrala, Sinha and Zoltners (1992)** find that profit responds more to the allocation rule than to the budget level. [02-mathematical-models.md](../01-foundation/02-mathematical-models.md) section 1.5 is that rule, applied to the gate's factors.
- **Gensch (1987) and Gilbride and Allenby (2004)** find organizational choice runs in two stages: a conjunctive screen on cutoffs, then compensatory trade-offs among the survivors. That is the shape of TCG's non-compensatory rule, and it is prior art for it. Swait's soft cutoffs, which test whether the screen holds, are open question 37 in [07-open-questions.md](../01-foundation/07-open-questions.md).

## Hockenmaier

Hockenmaier (2023) applies the same Dahlman triad to marketplaces. A marketplace sells the removal of transaction costs, and its stages each remove another. This is the closest vocabulary match found. TCG adds the coalition, per-cost thresholds, the two surplus conditions and Axiom III. **The separating prediction:** his stages imply a fixed order of cost removal. TCG predicts that no other spending counts while a cost reads past the buyer's limit, so a services marketplace whose enforcement cost reads past it should lead with guarantees before search.

## What is original and what is synthesis

Most of TCG is synthesis. The three costs are Dahlman's. The hold-up and governance apparatus comes from Williamson and the marketing transaction-cost line. The buying coalition comes from Webster and Wind and the Gartner work. "Lower the buyer's cost beyond price and you win" is in Cannon and Homburg and in purchase ease. The contract-value-against-acquisition-cost seller condition is Skok and Janz.

What looks original, as far as this search and the [2026-11-09 research review](./research-review-2026-11-09.md) reached, is narrower than the assembly. Four parts survive. First, the gate: two thresholds per cost, read in the payer's capacity, a position between them, and exclusion by any one cost. Non-compensatory screening is published in form (Gensch, Gilbride and Allenby), so what is new is its domain and its thresholds. Second, the expected position against the actual one: a seller can lower a cost and never show it, and the buyer never enters discovery. Third, Turnkey as a market condition that erodes with entry, so sales-assist arrives as the vendor count rises at constant buyer mix. Fourth, the seller's limit as directional: the cost of sale is spent against one cost, so the addressable market belongs to what the seller is built to absorb. Naming the motion for the tightest cost recombines Attewell, Ram and Sheth and Bunn. Seller spending earning its cost by raising value or by verification restates Walton and McKersie, Bagwell and Wernerfelt. "Price is a transfer" as sales doctrine is standard economics, though not standard sales practice.

Two weak points against these neighbors shaped Constitution 5.0. Challenger, SPIN and the anchoring literature contest the assumption that sellers cannot move willingness to pay. 5.0 answered by splitting value into realizable, recognized and realized, so the seller moves the buyer's estimate and not the value of a given offer, and [Value and Persuasion](./value-and-persuasion.md) carries the evidence and the objection that survives it. Axiom III's framing of future cost as exposure to what cannot be verified misses lock-in opportunism that holds under full information. 5.0 widened it to what a party cannot verify or enforce, and [Incomplete Contracts](./incomplete-contracts.md) carries Wathne and Heide's evidence. The open items stay in [07-open-questions.md](../01-foundation/07-open-questions.md).
