# Transaction Cost Economics

**Sources:**
- Coase, R.H. (1937). "The Nature of the Firm." *Economica*, 4(16), 386–405.
- Coase, R.H. (1960). "The Problem of Social Cost." *Journal of Law and Economics*, 3, 1–44.
- Dahlman, C.J. (1979). "The Problem of Externality." *Journal of Law and Economics*, 22(1). Page range to be verified against the journal record before any is quoted. Groups the costs of using a market into three classes, search and information, bargaining and decision, and policing and enforcement, and argues they share one root in imperfect information.
- Williamson, O.E. (1979). "Transaction-Cost Economics: The Governance of Contractual Relations." *Journal of Law and Economics*, 22(2), 233–261.
- Williamson, O.E. (1985). *The Economic Institutions of Capitalism*. Free Press.
- Williamson, O.E. (2009). Nobel Prize Lecture: "Transaction Cost Economics: The Natural Progression."

**Abstract.** Coase asked why firms exist at all if markets allocate well, and answered that using the price mechanism has costs. His 1960 paper lists the activities those costs pay for: finding whom to deal with, negotiating, drawing up the contract and checking that its terms are kept. Dahlman grouped the activities into three classes by kind. Williamson divided the same costs by time, into the ex ante costs of drafting, negotiating and safeguarding an agreement and the ex post costs of maladaptation, haggling and running the governance. He made *asset specificity* what creates the second: an investment locked to one relationship loses value if redeployed, so the party who made it faces hold-up once it is sunk. When specificity is high, the price mechanism alone fails, and governance structures (relational contracts, vertical integration, hostages) become preferable to spot transactions. The "Fundamental Transformation" describes how a market with many participants collapses to a bilateral monopoly once specific investments are made.

**Key claims:**
- Using the market costs something beyond price, and whether to use it at all is the first question (Coase).
- Those costs fall into three classes by kind: search and information, bargaining and decision, policing and enforcement (Dahlman).
- The same costs divide by time into ex ante and ex post, and asset specificity is what generates the ex post costs (Williamson).
- Asset specificity determines whether market or governance structure is preferred.
- **Frequency is the third selection property, alongside specificity and uncertainty.** Williamson (1979) crosses investment character (nonspecific, mixed, idiosyncratic) with frequency (occasional, recurrent) to select among four governance structures: market (classical contracting), trilateral (neoclassical, third-party adjudication), bilateral (relational, both parties autonomous), and unified (vertical integration). Nonspecific investment takes market governance at any frequency. Specific investment transacted occasionally takes trilateral. Specific and recurrent takes bilateral. Idiosyncratic and recurrent takes unified.
- The Fundamental Transformation: ex-ante competition becomes ex-post bilateral monopoly once specific investments lock the parties.
- Bounded rationality and opportunism make all complex contracts incomplete; governance handles what contracts cannot.
- "Slow down to speed up": safeguards agreed ex ante prevent catastrophic ex-post failures.
- Hostages (credible commitments) mitigate hold-up risk in incomplete contracts. By giving a hostage (such as performance guarantees, clawback clauses, or mutual resource commitments in the MIP), a vendor puts its own value at stake on delivery, which makes its commitment credible and the sanction at the next boundary mutual.

**What the sources say and what the framework adds.** The decomposition is cited, and the B2B reading of it is the framework's own.
- *Coase* owns the question Axiom I asks: whether to use the market, given that using it costs something.
- *Dahlman* owns the three-way split and the three names Axiom I uses. He also argued the three reduce to one cost, the resources lost to imperfect information. The framework accepts the common root and keeps the three apart because each yields to a different seller investment.
- *Williamson* owns the split by time, specificity, the Fundamental Transformation, and the specificity-by-frequency governance table.
- *The framework adds* the B2B enlargement of each cost (reachability for search, the buyer as a coalition for bargaining, "will it work here" for policing), a participation threshold per cost, the rule that the sale starts at the cost nearest its own threshold, and the reading of Williamson's ex ante and ex post line as the boundary between Axioms II and III.

**Supports in TCG:**
- **Axiom I — Law of Transaction Cost Composition** — Coase for the question and Dahlman for the three costs. The field names search, consensus and implementation map onto Dahlman's three. The B2B enlargement of each, and the participation threshold, are the framework's.
- **Axiom II — Law of Transaction Investment** — Williamson's ex ante costs, drafting, negotiating and safeguarding the agreement, are what today's investment by buyer or seller pays down.
- **Axiom III — Law of Future Cost** — direct theoretical basis. Williamson's ex post costs, and the asset specificity that creates them, are the future cost. Frequency is the governance-form selector.
- **The gate** (Axiom III corollary) — Williamson's result that nonspecific investment takes market governance at any frequency is the gate: with nothing specific sunk and a trial that verifies fit, market terms hold.
- **Governance Form** (Axiom III corollary) — the four structures above, mapped onto specificity and frequency in [05-governance-forms.md](../01-foundation/05-governance-forms.md). The MIP is the bilateral form.
- **Williamson Hold-Up** — direct.
- **Axiom III — Law of Future Cost, governance corollary** — bilateral hostages and relational contracts are the mechanism behind the stakes corollary and behind mutual sanction at each renewal boundary. A hostage makes the seller's commitment credible. Taken alone it moves future loss from buyer to seller, which the three-levers corollary counts as a transfer, except for what it changes in the seller's conduct and what it reveals about which seller it is.
