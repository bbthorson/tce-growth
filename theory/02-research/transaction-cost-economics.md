**Abstract.** Coase asked why firms exist if markets allocate well, and answered that using the price mechanism has costs. His 1960 paper lists what those costs pay for: finding whom to deal with, negotiating, drawing up the contract and checking that its terms are kept. Dahlman grouped them into three classes by kind. Williamson divided the same costs by time, into the costs of reaching an agreement and the costs of living under it. He made *asset specificity* the source of the second kind. An investment that is worth less outside one relationship exposes whoever made it once it is made. Klein, Crawford and Alchian named what is at stake in that exposure and said whose it is. Every claim builds on it, and the bullets at the end name the ones it backs directly.

**Key claims:**
- Using the market costs something beyond price (Coase).
- Those costs fall into three classes by kind: search and information, bargaining and decision, policing and enforcement (Dahlman). Dahlman also argued the three share one root, the resources lost to imperfect information.
- The same costs divide by time, before and after the agreement, and specific investment creates the costs that come after (Williamson).
- A market with many sellers becomes a relationship between two parties once either has made a specific investment. Williamson calls this the fundamental transformation.
- No party can foresee everything and a party may exploit a gap, so every complex contract is incomplete, and how the relationship is governed handles what the contract cannot.
- Safeguards agreed before the investment prevent the worst failures after it. A hostage, something a party loses if it defects, makes a commitment believable.
- How a relationship should be governed depends on how specific the investment is and how often the parties transact (Williamson 1979). A purchase with nothing specific in it takes ordinary market terms at any frequency. A specific investment between parties who deal repeatedly takes a relationship the two govern themselves. A specific investment made once takes an outside party to judge it.

## Hold-up: whose exposure it is

Klein, Crawford and Alchian name the quantity. When an investment is worth more inside one relationship than anywhere else, the difference is an *appropriable quasi-rent*: what the other side can take by threatening to walk away after the investment is made.

- **The exposure follows the investment.** The exposed party is whoever built what cannot be redeployed, on either side of the deal, whatever its size or sophistication.
- **Investing creates the exposure, and signing does not.** A contract signed later does not undo an exposure created earlier.
- **Three remedies exist:** own the other party, write a contract that protects against the squeeze, or hold the specific asset jointly. The authors treat the choice as a comparison of costs.
- **Teece adds who collects.** When an innovation depends on specialized complementary assets, whoever controls those assets captures the returns. A seller that builds a complement its buyer then owns has handed over the return with the work.

## What the sources say and what the framework adds

- **Coase** supplies the insight that using the market costs something. His alternative to the market is the firm, which is the buyer's "build". The buyer's question at Time0 adds a third answer, doing nothing. That answer descends from Akerlof's market that fails to form, and from search theory, where a searcher stops once one more look is worth less than it costs (Stigler 1961, McCall 1970, Weitzman 1979).
- **Dahlman** owns the three-way split. The Constitution's search, consensus and implementation descend from his three and differ in two places, which its premise states. The framework accepts his common root and keeps the three apart because each yields to different work by the seller.
- **Williamson** owns the split by time, specificity and the fundamental transformation. The Constitution reads his line between reaching an agreement and living under it as the line between Time1 and Time2, and calls a purchase with a specific investment in it *built-in*. His 1991 paper treats the institutional environment as parameters that shift the cost of every arrangement inside it, which is where claim 4's floor sits in the theory.
- **Heide, John and the marketing line** (Heide and John 1990 and 1992, Heide 1994, Rindfleisch and Heide 1997, Wathne and Heide 2000) carried transaction cost reasoning into buyer-supplier relationships: joint action, relational norms, safeguards for specific investment and remedies matched to forms of opportunism. Claims 11 and 12 draw on it almost in full. That literature does ask whether to use the market. Make-or-buy is its most common application, and Rindfleisch and Heide note attempts to measure transaction costs directly, among them Pilling, Crosby and Jackson's (1994) measure of anticipated costs, which no source list here carries.
- **The framework adds** the buyer's side of a B2B purchase read through the three costs, each with a home time. It names the motion for the kind of cost in the way, an idea that recombines Attewell, Ram and Sheth, and Bunn. It reads two things a leader can see, the workflow and the buyer's organization, as predictors of that cost. And it has expectations run backward, so a buyer prices a later cost at an earlier decision. [buyer-cost-neighbors.md](./buyer-cost-neighbors.md) sets out where its neighbors in marketing and sales practice agree and diverge.

## Supports in TCG

- **The premise.** Coase for the cost of using a market, and Dahlman for the three costs.
- **Claim 3.** Akerlof for buyers who cannot tell good sellers from bad and buy from no one. [costly-signals.md](./costly-signals.md) carries the remedy.
- **Claim 4.** Williamson's shift parameters are the floor: set outside any deal. They do not bind every seller alike, since claim 4 says the floor favors whoever has already paid it.
- **Claim 6.** A plug-in purchase is Williamson's nonspecific case, which takes market terms at any frequency. That is why a plug-in purchase needs standard terms and a trial and nothing more. A built-in purchase is the specific case.
- **Claim 9.** Williamson's point that safeguards agreed before an investment prevent the worst failures after it is the general form of claim 9. None of these sources is about discovery.
- **Claim 11.** The fundamental transformation is why a buyer cannot easily leave once it has built. Klein, Crawford and Alchian are why the exposure follows the investment, buyer or seller, and why a connector the seller reuses is not exposed and a mapping to one buyer's schema is. A seller that deploys engineers before signature is the exposed party in their terms. Teece is why control of what the buyer needs can capture the return without earning it.
- **Claim 12.** The staged plan with a standing review is Williamson's relationship that two parties govern themselves, for specific investment between parties who deal repeatedly. Their point that the exposure follows the investment is the reason the stages run in both directions: the seller's engineering is staged against the buyer's commitment so that neither side's unprotected exposure exceeds what a failed stage would cost. A built-in purchase that happens once is Williamson's occasional case, where the safeguard comes from outside the pair. A hostage alone moves a loss from buyer to seller. It adds to what the deal creates only through what it changes in the seller's conduct and what it shows about which seller is offering it.
