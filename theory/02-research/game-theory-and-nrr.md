# Game Theory and Net Revenue Retention

**Sources:**
- Axelrod, R. (1984). *The Evolution of Cooperation*. Basic Books. (Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, Tit-for-Tat strategy, Shadow of the Future.)
- Akerlof, G.A. (1970). "The Market for Lemons." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 84(3), 488–500.
- Nash, J.F. (1950). "Equilibrium points in n-person games." *PNAS*, 36(1), 48–49.
- Jensen, M.C. & Meckling, W.H. (1976). "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs, and Ownership Structure." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 3(4), 305–360.

**Abstract.** Axelrod's tournaments showed that in a game played once, defecting wins, and that in a game played many times cooperation can win, provided the future weighs enough against the gain from defecting now. Jensen and Meckling showed that an agent acts on its own payoff and not its principal's. The framework applies both to sales by analogy. A representative paid on signature plays one round, so overselling pays. Pay that waits on what happens after signature, which this file calls a vested commission, and a renewal the customer can decline, make the game repeat. None of these sources studies sales pay or software, so the application is the framework's argument and not a finding.

**Key claims:**
- A game played once makes defecting the winning move, and a repeated game can make cooperation win (Axelrod).
- Applied to sales: paying a representative on signature alone removes the future from the representative's game.
- Applied to renewal: a customer answers in kind when the gap between promise and delivery surfaces after implementation: first through stalled use and contraction, and through churn once an alternative clears the cost of leaving.
- Applied to pay: clawbacks and deferred commissions put the future back into the representative's game.
- Net revenue retention is read as the lagging sign of whether the repeated game is being played well.

**Supports in TCG:**
- **Claim 5.** The nearest basis for the pay half, by analogy. A representative paid on signature plays a game with one round, and overselling wins it. Pay that waits until the implementation confirms the scope makes the game repeat. Jensen and Meckling are why the representative's payoff is not the seller's, which is the third standing assumption. The Constitution adds a limit the game leaves out: deferred pay works only where the seller has defined what a complete discovery is, because otherwise the representative is paid on an outcome it cannot affect.
- **Claim 10.** The customer's answer to an oversold deal comes first as stalled use and contraction and later as an exit. Net revenue retention is the lagging reading of it.
- **Claim 12.** A renewal that either side can decline is what gives both sides something to lose. Axelrod's condition says the future has to weigh enough for cooperation to pay, and a built-in purchase that happens once has no such future.
- **Claim 3.** The same logic covers whoever recommends a seller. A channel or analyst paid on referral loses nothing when a bad recommendation passes.
