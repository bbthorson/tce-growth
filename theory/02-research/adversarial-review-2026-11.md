**Abstract.** On 2026-11-03 an external deep-research tool was asked to break seven of TCG's claims, and it returned a 38-page review. It scored two claims supported, three contested and two refuted. That score overstates the damage, because several refutations answer a shortened statement of a claim rather than the Constitution's own text. Five objections survive a reading against the Constitution, and each now has an entry in [07-open-questions.md](../01-foundation/07-open-questions.md). The review also brought about thirty sources the repository did not cite. This file records what it found, what the Constitution already answered, what it did not, and which of the review's own figures are not fit to cite.

**Key claims:**
- The non-compensatory rule matches the two-stage choice literature, which screens on cutoffs and then trades off among what survives. That literature is uncited prior art. The open test is whether buyers breach their own cutoffs when compensated elsewhere.
- Contracts can solve hold-up under full information without hostages. Option contracts and renegotiation design are a remedy the hostages corollary does not name.
- In a credence-goods experiment, liability moved market efficiency and verifiability barely did. That bears directly on the line that verification creates surplus.
- The bargaining cost floor mixes statute, which no buyer waives, with internal policy, which a sponsor can waive inside one deal.
- A seller can absorb too much of the buyer's investment. The buyer's own effort raises its valuation, its commitment and its later use.
- Health tech biases the home case toward high cost floors and toward stalls that are value problems rather than transaction costs.

**Supports in TCG:**
- **Axiom I — Law of Transaction Cost Composition** — two-stage choice backs the non-compensatory rule, and soft cutoffs give it a test. Buyer-side switching costs and AI buying agents bear on the Turnkey corollary.
- **Axiom II — Law of Transaction Investment** — health IT econometrics backs the cost floor corollary for statute. Private procurement narrows it for internal policy. The co-production evidence bounds seller absorption.
- **Axiom III — Law of Future Cost** — the mechanism-design and reputation work names remedies beside hostages, and the credence-goods work weighs liability against verification.
- **Second standing assumption** — the co-creation and anchoring work presses the objection already filed as open question 32.

## What the Constitution already answered

Three refutations miss because the query that prompted the review stated the claims more strongly than the Constitution does.

- **Costs add below their thresholds.** The review read Axiom I as forbidding any trade-off. The Constitution says the costs add in the buyer's condition, and that ease on one never buys back another above its threshold. Gensch and Gilbride and Allenby find exactly that shape in organizational buying: a conjunctive screen, then compensatory choice among the finalists. Public tenders that score price against quality trade product attributes, which are not transaction costs, so they do not bear on the rule.
- **A coalition faces three tests.** The review read "where the sale starts" as a claim that one buyer feels one cost. The Constitution says a one-person buyer approaches a single summed test and a large coalition approaches three separate ones. The figure the review used against the claim, that most larger SaaS firms run product-led and sales-led motions together, fits TCG's point that the motions are not strategies to choose between.
- **Hostages include staging and reputation.** The review attacked "only hostages work." The hostages corollary lists fees staged against delivery and the seller's referenceability, which covers part of Che and Sákovics's dynamic investment and Banerjee and Duflo's reputation result. The Constitution also says that what is settled in advance is the allocation, not the adaptation.

The review's charge that the value split is circular is the objection already filed as open question 32. Tuli, Kohli and Bharadwaj's finding that customers see requirements definition as part of the solution falls under "the seller can change the offer" in the second standing assumption. The review is right that this routing makes the assumption hard to falsify, and item 32 stays open.

## What survives

**Soft cutoffs (Axiom I).** Swait estimates stated cutoffs as penalties rather than walls, and finds respondents breach them when another attribute compensates. If buyers breach a transaction-cost cutoff the same way, the non-compensatory rule weakens from a gate to a steep penalty. Open question 37.

**Buyer-side lock-in holds Turnkey (Axiom I).** Burnham, Frels and Mahajan's procedural switching costs are specific investment the user sinks after adoption. "Nothing specific sunk" is true at entry and false a year later, so a Turnkey incumbent is protected by its own buyers' learning, which Axiom III predicts and the Turnkey corollary omits. Häubl and Trifts, and the 2026 vendor surveys of AI-assisted buying, suggest entry raises the seller's cost of being found more than the buyer's cost of searching. Open question 43, alongside 35.

**Internal policy is not a cost floor (Axiom II).** Miller and Tucker and the two Adjerid papers are the strongest evidence in the repository's home case that statute sets a floor no single deal moves. Bajari, McMillan and Tadelis find private buyers routinely set aside competitive bidding for complex projects, and Tuli and colleagues describe suppliers working around a buyer's internal barriers inside one deal. The corollary as written puts both under "the buyer's institutions." Open question 38.

**Seller absorption has a ceiling (Axiom II).** Norton, Mochon and Ariely show labor raises the builder's valuation. Arkes and Blumer show sunk effort raises the commitment to finish. Sabherwal, Jeyaraj and Chowa's meta-analysis puts user participation among the drivers of system use. A seller that absorbs all of the buyer's investment removes all three, and removes the buyer's hostage under Axiom III with them. Claim 6's falsifier assumes the effect of absorption is monotonic. Open question 39.

**Contracts without hostages (Axiom III).** Aghion, Dewatripont and Rey, and Nöldeke and Schmidt, show that renegotiation design and simple option contracts can induce efficient specific investment when information is observable to both parties and unverifiable to a court, which is the hostages corollary's own setting. Che and Hausch show that when investment is cooperative, ex ante contracts can be worth nothing. Banerjee and Duflo find fixed-price software contracts renegotiated after the fact in a large share of cases. Open question 40, which also bears on item 7.

**Liability against verifiability (Axiom III).** Dulleck, Kerschbamer and Sutter's laboratory credence-goods market found liability drove efficiency and verifiability had a minor effect. If that holds in enterprise software, verification lowers $\pi_p$ by less than the levers table in [02-mathematical-models.md](../01-foundation/02-mathematical-models.md) section 1.3 implies, and hostages carry more of the weight. Open question 41.

**"Verification creates it" overstates the math.** The recurring line from the 5.0 proposal said a discount moves the surplus and verification creates it. Section 1.3 already says more carefully that a discount can close a deal whose joint surplus is positive, and that verification pays only when it lowers the loss by more than it costs. Hirshleifer's point is the limit case: information that changes no decision only moves wealth between the parties, and the resources spent on it are lost. The line was qualified in every theory file that carried it on 2026-11-03.

**The home case is biased (cross-cutting).** Herzlinger describes a buyer split across clinicians, patients, administrators, payers and regulators. Under fee-for-service a tool that removes duplicate tests cuts the hospital's own billed revenue, which is a negative value to switch, not a cost above its threshold. Dranove and colleagues find hospital EMR adoption raised costs for years outside IT-intensive labor markets, and Greenhalgh's NASSS framework explains abandonment by complexity across many domains at once. A framework calibrated on health tech will read too many stalls as cost floors and bargaining thresholds. Open question 42.

## What the review got wrong or could not support

- **Vendor figures used as refutation.** Gartner's finding that self-serve buyers report more regret, the Semrush survey of AI-assisted buyers, the OpenView hybrid-motion share, the DerivateX AI-visibility benchmark and the ARR figures for individual AI companies are vendor material. None is cited here as evidence. Each is listed in the [provenance audit](./audits/citation-provenance-audit.md) as untraced.
- **An internal inconsistency.** The review gives Bajari, McMillan and Tadelis's share of private projects that skip open bidding as "nearly 50%" in one place and "82%" in another. The second seems to add direct negotiation and invited lists together. Open the paper before using either.
- **Mismatched links.** The link for Zhang et al.'s trusted-third-party model points to a different paper. The "Hauser et al." citation reuses Gilbride and Allenby's link. The link for Dane and Pratt (2007) points to a supplier-selection paper by other authors. Mora Cortez et al. (2026) in *Industrial Marketing Management* could not be confirmed.
- **An unsupported share.** The review says prior work anticipates 70 to 90% of TCG's assembly and gives no basis. Its list of prior art is useful, and several entries are already cited here: Liang and Huang, Teo and Yu, Barzel, Bakos and Burnham. Malone, Yates and Benjamin (1987) and Bunn (1993) were new, and [buyer-cost-neighbors.md](./buyer-cost-neighbors.md) now carries both with the prediction that separates each from TCG.
- **Unfalsifiability charges already on file.** The review's claim that thresholds are latent and that cost floors can be redrawn after the fact repeats open questions 24 and 34.
