# Predictions and Tests

**Version:** 2.1
**Purpose:** To list what only evidence can settle. Each of the twelve claims carries its own test in the [Constitution](./00-tcg-constitution.md). This file holds the objections to those claims that evidence would settle, and what a seller would have to record to run any of the tests. Nothing in the framework is measured.

## 1. What a seller would have to record

The claims' tests need five records that most sellers do not keep.

1. **Where each lost or stalled deal stopped.** The buyer never looked, looked and decided nothing, or signed and failed to adopt. Claims 1 and 2 are tested on this.
2. **The triage's readings, dated before the seller invests** and kept after the outcome is known: plug-in or built-in, the cost in the way, and whether each role's objection was collected before any group demonstration. Claims 6 and 7.
3. **Cost of sale split by what it bought.** Discovery and proof on one side, persuasion and discount depth on the other, beside the renewal. Claims 8 and 9, and open question 45.
4. **Scope changes after signature,** beside whether discovery was done before it and whether the staged plan was agreed before anyone built. Claims 9 and 12.
5. **At each renewal:** the triage's counts taken again, whether the implementation met the plan, use and contraction before any exit, and exits caused from outside, marked by a rule fixed beforehand. Claim 10.

Until those exist, every reading is a comparison between deals inside one book, and none predicts a close date.

## 2. Predictions waiting on data

Each is a claim the framework makes, or an objection to one, that writing cannot close.

### Axiom I, the market

**52. Turnkey erosion may be ordinary diffusion.** Claim 3 says a Turnkey market does not stay Turnkey. A rising vendor count should come before falling trial-to-paid conversion and the arrival of sales-assist teams. Diffusion predicts the same sequence for another reason: later adopters are larger organizations with more roles that can say no, and vendors move upmarket on their own. *Settled by:* category histories of vendor count and conversion, with the adopter's size held fixed. A public record exists: the date "contact sales" first appears on a product's archived pricing page, against the category's vendor count, with the segment held fixed by each plan's user limit.

**53. Sellers who publish their costs choose to.** Claim 3's test compares sellers who publish a commitment that costs them with matched sellers who do not. The sellers who publish are the ones whose costs are low, so matched sellers may not exist. *Settled by:* a publication the seller did not choose, such as a disclosure a regulator required.

**43. Lock-in on the buyer's side may hold a Turnkey market.** Claim 3 says a standard, a dominant brand or a channel with something at stake holds the search cost down as entrants arrive. Users also build configuration after they adopt (Burnham, Frels and Mahajan 2003), so a purchase that was plug-in at entry is built-in later, and claim 11 then protects the incumbent. *Settled by:* incumbent self-serve retention and trial conversion against entrant density across software categories, split by how much configuration a user builds.

**35. Whether AI buying agents break the link between entry and search cost.** Claim 3 says more entrants raise every buyer's search cost. Agents that search, negotiate and transact for a buyer could screen vendors so that more entrants need no more buyer effort (Shahidi et al. 2025), and interactive decision aids already move effort from the buyer's search to the seller's discoverability (Häubl and Trifts 2000). *Settled by:* buyer search effort per purchase tracked against vendor count, in categories where agents buy and where they do not.

**36. Who opens a market that a floor holds shut.** [01-motions.md](./01-motions.md) section 5 predicts that such a market opens only when someone whose return comes from many deals pays to lower the floor, and rarely through a seller in the middle of the market. Where only a belief holds buyers out, a single seller showing its own cost should open it. *Settled by:* category histories that date when buyers began buying at all, against what came first: a certification, a standard, a mandate, a marketplace, a contract vehicle, or a dominant seller's investment. Open question 34's certification dates are one such record. A second is federal: FedRAMP authorization dates joined to obligations by vendor, read for new agencies buying from each vendor after authorization, beyond what a lower price explains.

**38. The consensus floor mixes statute with internal policy.** Claim 4 puts the buyer's procurement and compliance rules outside any deal. Statute fits: state privacy law cut hospital adoption of electronic records in a way no single deal could undo (Miller and Tucker 2009, Adjerid et al. 2016). Internal policy may not. Private buyers set aside competitive bidding for complex projects (Bajari, McMillan and Tadelis 2009), and suppliers work around a buyer's internal barriers inside one deal (Tuli, Kohli and Bharadwaj 2007). *Settled by:* splitting the floor into outside rules and internal policy, then testing whether sponsors waive internal policy as a project grows more complex.

**3. Whether a built-in purchase adds roles that can say no.** Claim 2 says a complex organization often brings implementation with it. The reverse should hold too. The roles a deal ends with should exceed those mapped at the start, by the roles a step the product cannot perform falls on and the approvers the buyer's rules then require. A buyer that discovered a misrepresented fit should also ask more of the next seller in the category. *Settled by:* the count of roles logged at the start and at each stage, beside the divergent steps found. For the second, time to decision and approvers on repeat purchases from a different seller, against buyers with no failed implementation in the category.

**69. Work on a cost that is not in the way.** Claim 1 says the kind of cost in the way decides how to sell. Accounts that add up the buyer's costs predict a gain from lowering any of them (Cannon and Homburg 2001). Claim 1 predicts an interaction: lowering a cost that is not in the way does little until the one in the way falls. No evidence exists on the three costs. *Settled by:* survey or deal data of Cannon and Homburg's kind, read for the interaction. [buyer-cost-neighbors.md](../02-research/buyer-cost-neighbors.md) lists the other predictions that separate the claims from a neighbor.

### Axiom II, contracting

Claim 6's own test also separates it from a neighbor. The research on indecision recommends limited and reversible commitments for every stalled buyer (Dixon and McKenna 2022). Claim 6 says staging, guarantees and joint plans pay on a built-in purchase and add nothing where a trial shows the fit.

**39. A seller that carries the buyer's work may hit a ceiling.** The premise says a seller can carry part of each cost. Three findings point the other way past some share. Labor raises the builder's valuation (Norton, Mochon and Ariely 2012), effort already spent raises the commitment to finish (Arkes and Blumer 1985), and user participation drives later use of a system (Sabherwal, Jeyaraj and Chowa 2006). What the buyer builds is also what it stands to lose by leaving, so a seller that does everything removes it. *Settled by:* close rate and twelve-month use by the share the seller carried, comparing a pilot the seller funds in full, a pilot both fund, and an implementation the buyer carries.

**28. Whether people will state an objection one to one.** Claim 7 has the seller collect each role's objection apart. An outside review established that people will not write personal exposure on a document a seller holds, and the Bargaining Ledger split into a private map and a shared record in response. *Settled by:* running the map on live deals, and counting the roles whose occupant stated an objection privately against those the seller inferred.

**41. Liability may do what the framework credits to proof.** Claim 8 offers a commitment where a discount fails, and [02-mathematical-models.md](./02-mathematical-models.md) section 1.3 says verification raises what a deal creates. In a laboratory market for goods a buyer cannot judge, liability drove efficiency and verifiability had a minor effect (Dulleck, Kerschbamer and Sutter 2011). If enterprise software behaves the same way, commitments carry more weight than proof. *Settled by:* deals matched on what is built, comparing outcome-liability terms with verification pilots on realized value and renewal.

**7. Whether work done before signature beats a modular start.** Claims 9 and 12 pull discovery forward and settle who carries which risk before either side builds. *Settled by:* the test three reviews proposed. Stratify built-in deals by what is built and by the imbalance between what each side builds, randomize a front-loaded protocol against a modular one, and read win rate, deployment and retention together. The prediction is that a modular start wins where much of the misfit surfaces only in use, and loses little where the specification is static. [incomplete-contracts.md](../02-research/incomplete-contracts.md) carries the evidence.

**68. Price may act mostly through approval thresholds.** Claim 8's exception says a price taken under a spend threshold removes an approver. If that is the main way a price lowers a cost, a cheap tool that triggers a security review or a finance sign-off stalls under self-serve unless a marketplace or a pre-approved vendor list carries the consensus cost, and an expensive purchase with one approver moves quickly. Choosing the motion by contract value predicts the reverse in both cases (Skok, Janz). Public buyers bunch purchases under approval thresholds (Carril 2021, Palguta and Pertold 2017). Those two off-diagonal cases are untested. *Settled by:* close rate and time to decision on them. A public record exists in federal purchases around the simplified acquisition threshold, which rose in 2011 and again from 2018, split by purchase vehicle. An order from a pre-negotiated schedule carries less search and less checking than an open-market buy. Equal deterrence at the threshold in both would say ease on the other costs does not offset a consensus cost. Weaker deterrence on schedule orders would say it does, which is what the premise expects.

### Axiom III, renewal

**19. Uncertainty predicts governance weakly.** Meta-analyses of transaction cost economics (David and Han 2004, Carter and Hodgson 2006, Geyskens, Steenkamp and Kumar 2006) find that specific investment predicts how a relationship is governed, and that uncertainty predicts it weakly, with trust and joint problem-solving often standing in for formal safeguards. Claims 6 and 11, which turn on what is built, sit on the strong result. Discovery and the standing review, which answer what neither side can foresee, sit on the weak one. *Settled by:* the triage's counts validated against outcomes across a book of deals, and a stated account of when the norms of a relationship stand in for a staged plan. [tce-empirical-record.md](../02-research/tce-empirical-record.md).

**40. Which contract terms work in software deals.** Claim 12 names stages, stop points at a known price and a standing review. Theory supports rights of that kind where both sides can see what a court cannot (Aghion, Dewatripont and Rey 1994, Nöldeke and Schmidt 1995). Two results complicate it. Where both sides invest in the same thing, contracts written in advance may be worth nothing (Che and Hausch 1999), and fixed-price software contracts are often renegotiated after the fact (Banerjee and Duflo 2000). *Settled by:* dispute and termination rates compared across option, staged, fixed-price and time-and-materials contracts in built-in software deals. Two public sources are proposed and unchecked. One is the material contracts companies file with the SEC. The other is federal IT services contracts, comparing terminations under award-fee and incentive-fee contracts and under option years with terminations under fixed-price and time-and-materials contracts.

**33. Who drifts first after signature.** Claim 11 says the exposure follows the investment, buyer or seller. It makes no claim about whose conduct changes first. *Settled by:* a book of relationships coded for which party's conduct changed first, against what each side had built.

### Standing assumptions

**22. Opportunism as a design premise may be self-fulfilling.** Ghoshal and Moran (1996) argue that governance designed around opportunism produces it. The first assumption carries opportunism, the third says it describes and does not predict bad faith, and the Bargaining Ledger records each person's objection and its answer, not a verdict on the person. Parties also judge the intent behind a safeguard (Arikan 2020), which is why the ledger's map is private and only the arrangement is shared. *Settled by:* whether ledgers built on stated objections outperform defensive safeguards on comparable deals. [tce-empirical-record.md](../02-research/tce-empirical-record.md).

**32. Preferences may be constructed during evaluation.** The second assumption says value is set outside the sale. The strongest objection is that preferences for unfamiliar, complex purchases are built during the evaluation itself (Payne, Bettman and Schkade 1999), so the value of a change may have no stable referent before the sale. *Settled by:* evidence on whether anchoring and framing move the realized value of enterprise purchases, and not only stated estimates and prices. Any such test has to compare new accounts with new accounts, because a buyer shares its own data mostly with a seller it already buys from. [value-and-persuasion.md](../02-research/value-and-persuasion.md).

### The home case

**42. Health tech may be a biased home case.** Health tech has floors set by statute, a buyer split across clinicians, patients, administrators, payers and regulators (Herzlinger 2006), and outcomes that are hard to attribute. Under fee-for-service a tool that removes duplicate tests cuts the hospital's billed revenue, which is a change the buyer does not want and not a cost in its way. Adoption of electronic records also raised hospital costs for years outside labor markets rich in technical staff (Dranove, Forman, Goldfarb and Greenstein 2014). A framework built there may read a value problem as a consensus problem and overstate floors elsewhere. *Settled by:* the value reading in open question 66, and the same readings taken in a commercial software market with no statutory floors.
